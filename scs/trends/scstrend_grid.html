Methods
  1. Source. NOAA/NCEI Storm Events Database — every hail, tornado and thunderstorm-wind report. Only county-type records (CZ_TYPE = C) are used. Puerto Rico lies outside this grid.
  2. Extreme heat is not reports but the ECMWF ERA5 reanalysis (hourly, 0.25°): days whose maximum 2 m temperature, or maximum NWS heat index, reached the threshold, midnight to midnight local standard time. Each ERA5 cell counts its own days, and a box’s value is the mean over the ERA5 land cells (land–sea mask ≥ 0.5) whose centres lie in the U.S. inside it, each weighted by its land fraction (a partly-sea cell still runs cooler than the land beside it), so sea, lakes, Canada and Mexico are left out; a box with no such cell is not drawn. 1950 onward. The newest month’s last day lacks a few overnight hours until the following month is out, and is recomputed then. Before about 1960 the top heat-index thresholds (110 and 115 °F) are unreliable: early ERA5 has occasional one-hour humidity spikes that the heat index turns into extreme values. Contains modified Copernicus Climate Change Service information; ERA5 is produced by ECMWF.
  3. Heavy rain comes from PRISM daily precipitation (4 km, PRISM Group, Oregon State University) via ACIS: days whose 24-hour total (ending 12 UTC, to the nearest hundredth of an inch) reached 1, 2 or 3 inches. Each PRISM cell counts its own days, and a box’s value is the mean over the cells inside it that lie in the U.S. 1981 onward; the last six months are provisional. The gauge network behind PRISM changes over time, so trends carry some uncertainty from it. Heavy rain opens on 2002 because PRISM itself changes then: from 1 January 2002 its daily precipitation uses weather radar (NWS Stage II/IV, then MRMS from July 2020) to place rain between the gauges across the central and eastern U.S., and PRISM warns that this adds non-climatic variation to trends. Against long-record gauges, heavy-rain days jump at 2002 in every eastern state we checked (≥ 2″ days go from about 0.6 to 0.86 of the gauge count; no jump in the West, where radar is not used), so a trend that spans 2002 is inflated. Earlier years can still be chosen in the year boxes.
  4. Derecho counts, per box, the events (not days) on which a box has a qualifying wind report inside the coherent wind swath of a definitive derecho in the SPC archive of Squitieri, Wade and Jirak (2026) — NEXRAD-confirmed cases only, so the record begins in 1996, and all of them fall in April–August. Each event is dated by its swath’s start, so a storm crossing midnight counts once. A box the swath crossed without a report is not credited. Individual swaths are on the historical events page.
  5. Position each report. Its own BEGIN_LAT/BEGIN_LON where present; otherwise the center of the county that reported it (the average of its simplified boundary points, which can differ slightly from the true centroid for oddly shaped counties). The fallback matters almost entirely in 1993–1995, when hail and wind reports carry no coordinates at all.
  6. Bin into a box. A 2° × 2° grid over the lower 48 (24–50°N, 126–66°W; 390 boxes, about 260 of which ever see a hail, tornado or wind report).
  7. Apply the threshold. Each report is scored against the selected size or intensity cut. Thresholds are cumulative — a 2″ hailstone also counts as a 1″ one.
  8. Collapse to hazard days. Count distinct calendar dates on which a box saw at least one qualifying report. One storm producing forty reports in a box on one afternoon is one hazard day, which is what removes most duplicate-report inflation.
  9. Store those counts per box × year × month × threshold. Everything below is computed live in the browser from that table.
  10. Climatology map. Mean annual hazard days over the selected period. A year with no days is a real zero and is counted in the denominator — every box exists every year.
  11. Trend map. Ordinary least squares on the annual counts, reported per decade. A box is left gray unless it clears three coverage gates: enough years carrying a day (at least 8, or 30% of the period), those years spanning at least 70% of the period, and at least 2 of them in each half.
  12. Annual and monthly panels. The series for the single selected box, fitted with a 95% confidence band — and subject to the same three gates, so a box the map declines to fit does not get a slope here either.
  13. Smoothing. The two maps are drawn through a light Gaussian blur, sigma 2°, on by default and switchable to Raw. On this 2° grid that is exactly one box, and the kernel is cut off at two boxes (a 5 × 5 window). Each neighbour is weighted by the share of its box that is U.S. land, so a coastal or border box that counts few days because it holds little country does not pull its inland neighbours down. The county page uses the same 2° sigma, so the two are smoothed at a matched physical scale; it bites much harder there, because counties are roughly 0.3° while this field is already 2°-averaged by construction. A single 2° box holds few enough reports that its year-to-year value is noisy, and the blur pulls a stray box towards its neighbors without moving a coherent pattern. Three things it deliberately does not do: it does not fill gaps — only boxes that have a value contribute and the weights are renormalized by what was found, so edge boxes are not dragged towards zero by the empty ocean; it does not change which boxes are drawn, since a box the trend gates declined to fit stays gray either way; and it never touches the annual or monthly panels, which always show the unsmoothed series. Because it is a neighborhood average, the drawn value is not the box's own — hover reports both when they differ. Totals are not conserved. It also interacts with the white “exactly 0” shading: with smoothing on, a box is white only when its whole neighborhood is zero, so the zero boundary is drawn more conservatively than it really is. Switch smoothing off to see the true edge. Extreme heat is never smoothed: ERA5 is already a continuous gridded field. Heavy rain is smoothed at 1° rather than 2° by default (half a box: a 3 × 3 window), switchable to Raw, to match rain’s short correlation lengths while PRISM resolves real 4 km structure. For heavy rain only the trend map is smoothed; its climatology is always drawn raw, because a long-term mean is not noisy and a blur misplaces terrain and rain-shadow structure.
  14. Color scales. Set nationally from all boxes: the 98th percentile for climatology, and for trend the 95th percentile of the non-zero absolute values, symmetric about zero.

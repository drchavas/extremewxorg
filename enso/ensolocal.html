      About this outlook

      What does this page show?

      It turns NOAA’s El Niño and La Niña forecast into a plain-language outlook for any place on land: how this winter (December–February) and spring (March–May) are likely to differ from normal in temperature, precipitation (rain and snow together) and snowfall, for each season and each month.

      Is this a forecast?

      Yes, in that it uses past data to estimate how things may be different from normal in the future. This is similar to the forecast statement “it will warm up this summer”: different on average (because past data shows that summer months are usually the warmest). This is not a regular daily weather forecast: we do not know what the weather will be like on any specific day.

      What do “a little” and “a lot” mean?

      They describe the change over the whole season. Temperature: 0–1 °F is near normal, 2–4 °F a little, 5 °F or more a lot. Precipitation and snow: up to 10% is near normal, 20–30% a little, 40% or more a lot.

      Where does the El Niño forecast come from?

      From the NOAA Climate Prediction Center’s official outlook for the Relative Oceanic Niño Index, issued each month around the second Thursday. This site uses its most likely (median) December–February value and is updated after each new issue.

      Why can my town’s typical temperature look a little off?

      The numbers describe a 2° box (about 200 km across), not a single weather station, so in mountains or along coasts the box average can differ from the town itself. The changes from normal are more representative than the typical values.

      Who makes this site?

      Dan Chavas, Professor of Atmospheric Science at Purdue University, as part of extremewx.org, with support in part from the U.S. National Science Foundation. The data and methods are described on the Science view.

      How is the outlook made?

      For every 2° box on Earth (about 200 km across), each month’s temperature, precipitation and snowfall from 1979 to 2025 (the ERA5 reanalysis from ECMWF) is fitted against that year’s El Niño or La Niña strength, NOAA’s Relative Oceanic Niño Index (RONI) for December–February, with one straight line for El Niño and one for La Niña and the long-term trend removed. The outlook reads those lines at the December–February strength that NOAA’s Climate Prediction Center currently forecasts.
